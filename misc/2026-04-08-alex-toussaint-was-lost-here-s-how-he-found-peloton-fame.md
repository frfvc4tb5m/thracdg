# Alex Toussaint Was Lost. Here's How He Found Peloton Fame.

This story is part of our ongoing “First Steps” series, where we share extraordinary stories of men who transformed their bodies, minds, and lives with a focus on the first steps it took them to get there (because, after all, nothing can change without a first step!). Read all of the stories here.

[Was Lost](https://leanorbit.dns0663.com/blog/0166740135.html)

SOMETIMES A FIRST step involves more than one first step. That’s how it was for the acclaimed Peloton instructor, Alex Toussaint.

His early life didn't mirror the way it is now. His frustrated parents dropped him off at military school at age 12. “Up to that point, I’d been a total disaster,” he writes writes in his new book, Activate Your Greatness. Kicked out of every school he’d been in, he says he had no respect for authority figures—and no respect for himself.

After what he told us was another “decade of disaster"—one that included getting kicked out of military school—he hit rock bottom. He was living at his parents’ house, emotionally numb and financially broke. The first step toward change—“the one that led directly to the second one,” as he puts it—“was getting kicked out of my parents’ house and hearing the words my father said to me: ‘you’ll never be shit in your life.’ Instead of crushing me mentally and emotionally, it kind of lit a fire internally. Deep down inside, I knew I wasn’t what my father was saying. But I had no direction or purpose.”

Direction and purpose didn’t come overnight. But Toussaint was motivated to prove his dad wrong. The night he was kicked out, a friend of Toussaint’s let him sleep in a makeshift bed on his floor. The next morning was a true wake-up call. Toussaint realized he could stay miserable or try to get off the ground and apply himself.

Casting around for work led him to a job as a janitor at the spin studio, Flywheel. Instead of just doing the job and going home, he decided to change his approach. He’d “outwork his job description,” he says, applying intent and purpose to everything he did.

As he was cleaning, though, he kept noticing what the Flywheel instructors were doing—the energy they were putting out and the energy they were getting back. He thought about what he’d do if he were on that stage, teaching. “I was inspired by movement of other people, and could only imagine what I was able to accomplish if I was to move the same way,” he says.

For the first time in his life, he was building confidence, giving out, and getting notice back. Flywheel cofounder Ruth Zukerman knew who he was, appreciated what he was doing. One day, in the parking lot of the studio, he told her he wanted to be an instructor.

"FEAR is something that we’re always going to experience as HUMAN BEINGS, and it’s your DUTY to CHANGE your PERSPECTIVE on it."

The official turning point in his life, he says, was when she asked if he was serious, and then said she’d train him. He was ready, he says: “Behind the scenes, I was practicing and doing the ‘invisible work,’ trying to put myself in a position to stay ready so if Ruth did provide me an opportunity, I was able to execute it to the best of my abilities, versus her having to coach me every step along the way.”

The first few pedal strokes he did on a bike, he says, changed everything. “Physically, we were going nowhere, but emotionally and mentally, it opened up a whole new perspective on life which allowed me to go so many other directions,” he says. “I knew I had something in me. I just didn’t know how to activate it. I didn’t know what direction to take it. It was the movement of the bike which started to really get the internal frequency going.”

He honed his authentic, inspiring, challenging style at Flywheel. When Zukerman heard that Peloton was starting up, she saw it as a life-changer and pointed Toussaint there, where his career took off.

While Toussaint credits Zukerman for helping him change his life, he also stresses that “greatness is not activated by others. It’s done by ourselves.” Some of his tips for activating power in yourself:

Let Your Accountability Partners Help You

SURROUND YOURSELF WITH people who believe in you and hold you accountable. Early on at Flywheel, his fellow janitors expressed faith in his ability to lead class. Today, he surrounds himself with people “who do everything that’s needed that nobody else gets to see,” he says. You need to surround yourself with people who uplift you. And, he says, “have people in your corner who will tell you the things you don’t want to hear, but need to hear. Some of my friends understand me better than I understand myself."

Check Your Frequency

EVERY MORNING, TOUSSAINT takes his time getting out of bed, taking a “moment of gratitude for literally the smallest of all things. It provides so much more of a positive start to the day versus just waking up and being like, ‘oh, I have to go to work.’ No, I get to wake up. I get to move my body.” Either first thing or in his morning workouts, he inhales confidence and exhales doubt with three breaths in and three breaths out. “This shakes out all that toxic frequency—that doubt and insecurity that we naturally wake up with—first thing in the morning,” he says.

Fail More

"I'M ALL ABOUT fear,” he says. To conquer his fear of heights, for instance, he jumped out of an airplane to let himself know that he “could have the mental switch to flip that fear and overcome it.” It’s not so much about getting rid of fear, he explains. It’s about learning to work with it. “Fear is something that we’re always going to experience as human beings, and it’s your duty to change your perspective on it, because on the other side of fear is growth. For instance, if you’re able to be comfortable to fail, you’re able to succeed every single day. Failure is the first attempt in learning. The more you fail, the more you learn; the more you learn, the more you grow.”

Use the 48-Hour Rule

STUFF HAPPENS. AND when it does, Toussaint gives himself 48 hours to deal with it. Instead of sweeping it under the rug, “I will embrace my emotion and figure out a way to align with it to move it out of the way, but definitely not dismiss it,” he says. By acknowledging and understanding it, he says, “it doesn’t block my road to greatness. After that 48 hours, I continue to push on. I’ve tried to implement that for the good and the bad things, because I don’t want to live by the cheers and die by the boos.”

Stay Coachable

"I TRULY BELIEVE that Ruth saw me every single day with a mop in my hand, but with the mindset and a perspective to stay coachable. When she trained me for two weeks, she knew I was going to listen. She knew I was going to be accountable and do my best in this opportunity. I think that’s the main reason she gave me an opportunity. And that was the main reason I committed to it so hard and so well. If you’re coachable, you’ll always have a place to fail forward because you’ll always be willing to learn. If you stay coachable, your pride and ego will never get in the way of your greatness.”
