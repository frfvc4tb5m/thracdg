# How to Stop Coping and Start Living

The first time I met a patient I’ll call Joe, a software engineer in his mid-50s, it was clear he didn’t need my help in figuring out why he wasn’t happy with his life. “I hate my job, and that sums it up,” he said. But he wasn’t sure what to do about it. He spent his time away from work trying to find ways to cope. He’d go running, attend concerts with his wife, and try to avoid thoughts about work by binge-watching Netflix. He even went to a yoga retreat and learned to breathe and move to let go of his stress. But eventually, he realized he was repeating the same cycle—stress, cope, repeat—and thought: There has to be something better.

Don’t get me wrong; most of the coping strategies Joe was using can be really beneficial for mental health—even a little TV. But coping can do only so much. It doesn’t usually fix what’s causing the distress in the first place. People can get stuck just coping for years. If coping is like a life jacket that you’d drown without, you’re probably in an unhealthy coping loop.

[Stop Coping and Start](https://pigmentlab.jcesqc8.com/blog/9309474126.html)

A different type of coping, called active coping, can help you find your way out. In psych speak, active coping is a stress-management technique in which you work directly to control a stressor with specific behavior. In other words, active coping strategies help you face challenges head-on instead of just trying to deal with the stress they keep dumping on you. This helps you feel better, solve problems, build resiliency, and make positive changes. Some men spend time with a therapist figuring out these strategies, but that’s not required. The process Joe and I used can help you, too.

Think about life before you had to cope so much

Joe told me he’d started waking up with a feeling of dread, thinking, Here we go again. All day, he looked forward to doing whatever coping technique he’d planned. I helped him think about times he didn’t feel the need to cope just to survive. He realized that was during the height of the pandemic, when he was working remotely and had more control over his time. That enabled us to develop an active coping strategy that was focused on changing his schedule.

If you have no idea how to figure out what you need, start by looking at what’s stressing you out. Aim your strategies at any part of that stress you can have control over.

Figure out why you’re not already doing active coping

Joe understood that if he wanted that stress and dread to go down, he should schedule a meeting with his manager about changing his schedule. But he hadn’t done it. This is where a lot of people get hung up—taking the first step toward making that active coping strategy happen. Often you hesitate because there’s a thought that’s blocking you. To get unstuck, you need to identify that thought. Then ask yourself how true that thought actually is—is there objective evidence to support your reasoning? When you see that it’s not as big of a roadblock as you’d imagined, it’s much easier to take action.

In my practice, I often see the following thoughts get in the way. See if any of them sound familiar, and pay attention to how interrogating your thought process can lead to a solution:

• “It’s more comfortable to vent than to do something to rock the boat.” Is staying where you are really comfortable? Probably not, or you wouldn’t be wishing for change. Discomfort isn’t always negative; it’s often just our mind’s way of telling us it’s time for something to shift.

• “I don’t think taking action is going to work.” Can you really do absolutely nothing to move the needle? If you’re in a relationship where you don’t feel supported, you can’t make your partner appreciate you overnight. But there’s always something you have control over. For instance, working on how well you listen and how well you express yourself can open lines of communication that help you get what you need from each other. (If you feel that your listening skills can use a boost, as so many of ours can, here's a plan that helps you become better at listening.)

• “If I use these strategies, everything’s going to fall apart.” That’s what was holding Joe back. He was worried his manager would think he wasn’t committed, and he feared he might lose his job. Was that true? He recognized he was an experienced engineer who was marketable in his field. If the meeting didn’t go well, he could probably get a job somewhere he’d be happier. That realization empowered Joe to set up the meeting. It went better than he anticipated, and he’s now working from home one or two days a week.

Of course, things aren’t always going to go well. You have to be prepared to make radical change—to actually leave the job in this case—if they don’t. As frightening and unsettling as the prospect of that change may be, it could lead to a positive outcome rather than you staying in the dread/cope cycle.

[Coping and Start Living](https://allpepay.com/)

I’m not going to say that if you use active coping, you’re never going to need the runs, the yoga, and other coping skills. (You will.) But if you’re coping, coping, coping and still hanging on by your fingernails, it’s a good time to get more active with your approach.
