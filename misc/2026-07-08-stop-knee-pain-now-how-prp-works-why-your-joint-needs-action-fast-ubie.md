How PRP for Knee Pain Works

Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) therapy is an emerging, minimally invasive treatment for knee pain that uses your body's own healing factors. In PRP for knee pain, a small sample of your blood is processed to concentrate platelets—cells rich in growth factors—and then injected into your knee joint. Those growth factors may help reduce inflammation, promote tissue repair, and possibly slow cartilage degeneration.

What Is PRP?

You have red blood cells, white blood cells, plasma, and platelets circulating in your blood.

Platelets normally help with clotting and wound healing by releasing growth factors.

In PRP, we spin your blood in a centrifuge to separate and concentrate platelets into a small volume of plasma.

That concentrated "platelet soup" is injected into the damaged knee area.

How PRP for Knee Pain Works

Blood Draw and Preparation

A clinician draws 15–60 mL of your blood (about one to two standard tubes).

The blood is spun at controlled speeds to isolate the platelets from red and white cells.

Who May Benefit from PRP for Knee Pain?

Not every knee problem is suited for PRP. Ideal candidates often have:

Mild to moderate osteoarthritis (grade I–III)

Tendon or ligament injuries around the knee

Persistent knee pain despite physical therapy, NSAIDs, or cortisone shots

No active infection or blood-clotting disorders

PRP is less likely to help in advanced osteoarthritis (bone-on-bone) or if you have severe joint deformity.

Evidence from Credible Sources

Studies in reputable journals show mixed but promising results:

A 2019 meta-analysis in the American Journal of Sports Medicine found PRP provided better pain relief than hyaluronic acid injections at 6–12 months.

Randomized trials suggest PRP may delay joint replacement surgery in early osteoarthritis.

Safety data indicate few serious side effects, since PRP uses your own blood.

While more large-scale research is needed, current evidence supports PRP as a potential option when conservative treatments fail.

Why Your Joint Needs Medical Next Steps

Even if PRP for knee pain seems appealing, your knee pain may signal underlying issues that require a comprehensive evaluation:

Cartilage damage or bone bruises visible on MRI

Mechanical problems (e.g., meniscal tear, ligament instability)

Systemic conditions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, gout)

Infection risk if the knee is red, hot, and swollen

A proper diagnosis guides the right treatment. Skipping imaging or a specialist consult could delay care for a treatable problem.

What to Expect at Your Doctor Visit

Medical History & Physical Exam

Review onset, pattern, and severity of knee pain

Assess walking, bending, and stability

Imaging Studies (if needed)

X-rays for arthritis severity

MRI for soft-tissue injuries or cartilage status

Conservative Treatments

Physical therapy focusing on muscle strength and flexibility

Weight management to reduce joint load

Bracing or orthotics for alignment support

Injection Options

Corticosteroids for short-term relief

Hyaluronic acid for lubrication

Follow-Up Plan

Track pain, function scores, and side effects

When to Seek Immediate Help

Contact a doctor promptly if you experience:

Severe, sudden knee pain after trauma

Inability to bear weight on the knee

High fever with red, hot, swollen knee (possible infection)
