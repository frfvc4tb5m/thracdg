# Basophils Blood Test Meaning - Hone Health Biomarkers

What are Absolute Basophils?

Basophils are white blood cells produced in the bone marrow. They make up less than 1% of your total white blood cell count, anywhere from 0-200 basophils per microliter (uL) of blood.1

Despite their small numbers, they act as amplifiers of immune signaling. When your body encounters an allergen or pathogen, basophils release histamine and heparin. These chemicals trigger inflammation and increase blood flow to areas where the immune system is responding.

Physicians measure both absolute basophil count and basophil percentage in a blood test to determine whether basophil levels themselves are shifting, or if the ratio of white blood cells has changed (i.e. neutrophils or lymphocytes).

If both basophil percentage and absolute count are both elevated, then basophil levels are truly elevated, which may point to conditions like allergic reactions, inflammation, hypothyroidism, or blood disorders such as chronic myeloid leukemia.

If the percentage of basophils is elevated but the absolute count is normal, it may indicate that other white blood cell populations — such as neutrophils or lymphocytes — have decreased, making basophils appear proportionally higher even though their actual numbers haven’t changed.

Low Absolute Basophil Levels (Basopenia)

Low basophil levels can occur after the immune system has already responded to an infection or allergen, though persistently low levels may also be linked to hyperthyroidism, corticosteroid use, or bone marrow suppression. Symptoms are caused by the underlying condition, rather than low basophils themselves.

How Hone Treats Out of Range Absolute Basophils

After your blood test, your Hone physician will evaluate your absolute basophil count alongside your basophil percentage, symptoms, medical history, and other biomarkers to understand what may be driving the change. Because basophils often rise or fall in response to other conditions, treatment focuses on identifying and addressing the underlying condition.

Antihistamines* — to manage allergic-driven basophilia and reduce histamine release

Corticosteroids* — to reduce inflammation and lower basophil activity

Antibiotics* — to treat confirmed bacterial infections
