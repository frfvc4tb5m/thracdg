Reviewed by Yoshinori Abe, MD

Prediabetes is a common, often silent condition where blood sugar rises above normal due to insulin resistance and beta cell strain. The good news: it is reversible. Left untreated, prediabetes can progress to type 2 diabetes and increase the risk of heart, kidney, nerve, and eye disease.

Evidence-based steps to reverse prediabetes include:

Losing 5 to 7 percent of body weight

Getting at least 150 minutes of weekly activity, plus strength training

Following a Mediterranean-style, high-fiber diet

Improving sleep and managing stress

Considering metformin for higher-risk cases

Rechecking A1C every 6 to 12 months

Because prediabetes rarely causes obvious symptoms, many people don't realize their blood sugar is drifting upward until real damage begins. Explanation

If you've been told you have prediabetes, you're not alone. More than 1 in 3 adults have prediabetes, and most don't even know it. The good news? Prediabetes is reversible in many cases. But ignoring it can lead to type 2 diabetes, heart disease, nerve damage, and other serious health problems.

Let's break down what prediabetes really means, why your body starts resisting insulin, and what medically approved steps can help you turn things around.

What Is Prediabetes?

Prediabetes is a condition where your blood sugar levels are higher than normal, but not high enough to be diagnosed as type 2 diabetes.

It's usually identified through blood tests:

Fasting blood sugar: 100–125 mg/dL

A1C: 5.7%–6.4%

Oral glucose tolerance test: 140–199 mg/dL after 2 hours

If nothing changes, many people with prediabetes develop type 2 diabetes within 5–10 years. But progression is not inevitable.

Why Your Body Resists Insulin

To understand prediabetes, you need to understand insulin.

Insulin is a hormone made by your pancreas. Its job is to help move sugar (glucose) from your bloodstream into your cells, where it's used for energy.

In prediabetes, two key problems develop:

1. Insulin Resistance

Your muscle, liver, and fat cells stop responding properly to insulin. Think of insulin as a key and your cells as locked doors. In insulin resistance, the locks get rusty.

As a result:

Sugar stays in your bloodstream

Your pancreas produces more insulin to compensate

Over time, the pancreas struggles to keep up

2. Beta Cell Strain

The pancreas has specialized cells (beta cells) that produce insulin. Constant overproduction exhausts these cells.

Why Does Insulin Resistance Happen?

Several scientifically supported factors contribute to prediabetes:

✅ Excess Body Fat (Especially Abdominal Fat)

Fat around the abdomen is metabolically active. It releases inflammatory chemicals that interfere with insulin signaling.

✅ Physical Inactivity

Muscle tissue helps absorb glucose. When you're sedentary, glucose stays in your bloodstream longer.

✅ Genetics

Family history increases risk, but lifestyle still plays a major role.

✅ Poor Sleep

✅ Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

Signs and Symptoms of Prediabetes

Most people with prediabetes have no obvious symptoms.

Sometimes you may notice:

Increased thirst

Frequent urination

Fatigue

Blurry vision

Darkened skin patches (acanthosis nigricans), especially around the neck or armpits

Medically Approved Next Steps for Prediabetes

The most powerful treatments for prediabetes are lifestyle-based — and they are strongly supported by large clinical trials.

1. Aim for Modest, Sustainable Weight Loss

Losing just 5–7% of your body weight can significantly reduce your risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

For example:

If you weigh 200 pounds, losing 10–14 pounds can make a major difference.

You do not need extreme diets. Sustainable change is more effective than rapid weight loss.

2. Increase Physical Activity

Exercise makes your cells more sensitive to insulin — almost immediately.

Evidence-based targets:

At least 150 minutes per week of moderate exercise(brisk walking counts)

Strength training 2–3 times per week

Even walking 30 minutes a day, five days a week, significantly lowers progression to diabetes.

If you're new to exercise, start slowly. Consistency matters more than intensity.

3. Improve Your Diet (Without Going to Extremes)

You don't need to eliminate all carbs. Focus on quality and balance.

Emphasize:

Vegetables (especially non-starchy)

Whole grains

Lean proteins (fish, poultry, beans)

Healthy fats (nuts, seeds, olive oil)

High-fiber foods

Limit:

Sugary drinks

Refined carbohydrates (white bread, pastries)

Highly processed foods

Excess saturated fats

A Mediterranean-style eating pattern has strong evidence for improving insulin sensitivity.

4. Prioritize Sleep

Aim for 7–9 hours per night.

Poor sleep:

Increases insulin resistance

Raises hunger hormones

Makes weight management harder

If you snore heavily or feel excessively tired during the day, ask your doctor about sleep apnea screening.

5. Manage Stress

Chronic stress keeps blood sugar elevated.

Helpful strategies:

Daily walks

Mindfulness or meditation

Breathing exercises

Counseling or therapy when needed

Even 10 minutes a day of stress reduction can help regulate cortisol levels.

6. Consider Medication (If Appropriate)

For some people, lifestyle changes alone may not be enough.

Doctors may prescribe metformin, especially if you:

Are under 60

Have a BMI over 35

Have a history of gestational diabetes

Have rising A1C levels despite lifestyle changes

Metformin improves insulin sensitivity and reduces liver glucose production. It is generally well-studied and safe but should only be taken under medical supervision.

What Happens If Prediabetes Is Ignored?

It's important not to minimize prediabetes.

Without intervention, risks increase for:

Type 2 diabetes

Heart disease

Stroke

Kidney disease

Nerve damage

Vision problems

However, progression is not guaranteed. Many people return their blood sugar to normal ranges with consistent lifestyle changes.

How Often Should You Be Tested?

If you've been diagnosed with prediabetes:

Get your A1C checked every 6–12 months

Monitor blood pressure and cholesterol

Track weight and waist circumference

Regular follow-up helps catch worsening blood sugar early.

A Calm but Clear Bottom Line

Prediabetes is serious — but it is also a powerful warning sign.

Your body is signaling that insulin resistance is developing. The encouraging part? At this stage, the condition is often reversible.

Small, steady changes can:

Improve insulin sensitivity

Reduce inflammation

Protect your pancreas

Lower your long-term health risks

You do not need perfection. You need consistency.

You should speak to a doctor promptly if you experience:

Final Thoughts

Prediabetes is common. It is manageable. And in many cases, it is reversible.

Understanding why your body resists insulin gives you the power to respond effectively. Focus on steady improvements in movement, nutrition, sleep, and stress management. Ask your doctor whether medication is appropriate for you.
