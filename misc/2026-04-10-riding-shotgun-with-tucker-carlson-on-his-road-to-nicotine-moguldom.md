“OF ALL THE underrated musical acts in American history,” Tucker Carlson says as we pull onto a two-lane in southwestern Maine, “can I just say that Nitty Gritty Dirt Band is probably at the top of the list?”

This is not the first take I expect from Carlson when we meet near his home in the middle of October. In the weeks leading up to our meeting, I’d immersed myself in all things Carlson, and I had read that he is, in name and deed and no particular order, a racist, misogynistic, anti-Semitic, ethno-​nationalist, permanently aggrieved, let-me-talk-to-your-manager Karen. I was expecting an angry person. Unhinged. A personality and presence befitting “one of the more extraordinary trajectories into insanity I’ve ever witnessed,” a former friend of his told me. What I was not expecting was the Grateful Dead Dad settling into the driver’s seat of a Chevy pickup, shaggy-haired, smooth-skinned, and dressed in a puffy green L.L.Bean vest he’s worn since 1983. We were setting off for his family’s shooting and fishing camp, about a 40-minute drive from where he records his blockbuster podcast, The Tucker Carlson Show, so he had swapped out his customary Birks for chukkas and fired up an aggressively middle-aged-dad playlist that complemented his red-and-blue flannel shirt. He does not wear a seat belt, and he keeps his eyes on the road.

I’m here to talk to Carlson about his year-old line of nicotine pouches, Alp, which he launched at the end of 2024 and which makes up a small but growing fraction of a $5 billion (and rapidly expanding) global market. Carlson loves these pouches the way that most people love…well, I’m not sure I know anyone who loves anything as much as Carlson loves these pouches. From his first stirrings each morning to his final flickers at night, he has a thin white nugget nestled firmly between gum and lip, powering his body, supercharging his mind, and delivering him from the fatigue that might weigh down another 56-year-old building his own business. He is a nicotine evangelist, if not the nicotine evangelist, and over the past five years, he sort of became synonymous with nicotine pouches just as nicotine pouches sort of became synonymous with an inchoate online movement that some have taken to calling the manosphere.

His constant praise of the pouches—and in particular his ill-fated love of a brand called Zyn—has given him currency with a new generation of men who have barely heard of Jon Stewart, much less Paul Begala, and landed him on the podcasts of shit-stirrers you know (Joe Rogan, Theo Von) and ones you probably don’t (the Nelk Boys, a knucklehead collection with 8.4 million YouTube subscribers who have also hosted Andrew Tate, Conor McGregor, and the Donalds Trump). To this younger audience, he became “Tucker CarlZyn,” a cool uncle and something of an edgelord, quick with DEI jokes and world-wise insights and asides about vaccine safety (really?) and Tate being misunderstood (go on…) and the existence of aliens and demons (okaaaay). Unlike in his latter days on Fox, all stone-faced and seething with hemorrhoidal contempt, he’s clearly having a ball on these shows. He’s at once totally at ease in his body and visibly wired, and with his tendency to speak in winding paragraphs and his joyful, high-pitched, Amadeus-​ian laugh, he’s like the guy at a party who seems like a good hang until he starts talking about the deep state.

CARLSON HAS LOVED nicotine his entire adult—and young adult—life, starting with cigarettes in 1983 as a boarding school teen before adding Copenhagen dip to his daily rotation. “For the majority of my life, I smoked Camel regulars, but the problem with Camels for me was you almost couldn’t smoke enough—you would always be burning another, right? And you could never really get full. It’s almost like eating candy.”

He quit smoking a decade ago and took up nicotine gum and dip and whatever else he could fit in his mouth until a fateful family gathering in 2020. “I was having dinner at home, and one of my daughters brought her boyfriend. He goes, Have you heard about nicotine pouches? I had never heard of it, so I tried one and I thought it was delicious.”

That Carlson, a terminally off-line Gen X’er, had never heard of nicotine pouches isn’t surprising. Native to northern Europe, the category was first introduced to the U.S. market in 2016 and, after a sleepy few years on shelves, exploded in popularity during the pandemic, mostly through Gen Z influencers hyping up the (alleged!) energy-boosting, mind-​enhancing benefits of the product through viral videos shared and consumed in peak-lockdown boredom. The small white pouches, which look like steroidal Tic Tacs and also go by lip pillows, cushies, deckys, and upper deckys, contain nicotine salts and artificial flavorings. Without the tar and fewer of the carcinogens that make cigarette smoking so deadly, pouches became a less unhealthy way of ingesting nicotine. Monthly sales of pouches tripled between 2021 and 2024, with male consumers leading the charge and Zyn, a Swedish brand acquired by Philip Morris International in 2022, driving the growth.

There were other brands vying for the precious real estate in Americans’ mouths—On!, Rogue, and Velo were early competitors—but it was pretty much Zyn from the jump. Its consumers, or more accurately its fans, created an Internet subculture that some would call the Zynternet, and they minted all manner of sticky memes and punny neologisms (Zynbabwe, Zynpocalypse) that hyped up the pouches and turned a generation of exceedingly online users on to nicotine. Into this mix came Carlson: “I was an extremely enthusiastic user of Zyn for three or four years,” he says now, and he let everyone know it. In early 2023, on the Nelk Boys’ podcast, Carlson shared an imagined (and unsolicited) ad campaign for the company: “Zyn is not a sin. Zyn is a lifesaving medical product that enhances male vitality and mental acuity. Zyn is like the hand of God reaching down and massaging your central nervous system.” In a related video that circulated on social media, the Boys brought Carlson out to a field and dangled a merry-go-round-size tin from a hovering helicopter to his astonished delight. He looked like he was witnessing the rapture. “This is the greatest day!” he cried, salvation descending from the heavens like a meme-culture messiah.

This is the part where I admit that I do not care for nicotine pouches. I have tried different flavors and strengths, and they’ve left me dizzy and nauseous and reminded me of vomiting in my parents’ front yard following a disastrous teenage dip-swallowing incident. Riding shoulder to shoulder with Carlson, I asked him to tell me exactly what it is that makes these things so special. “I love the taste,” he says. “I love the convenience. And above all, I loved the full nicotine saturation of it. Maybe for the first time in 25 years, I felt like, oh, I have enough nicotine. And you can use it all day, from the second you wake up until the second you fall asleep. And I do.”

I tell Carlson that it sounds like he’s addicted.

“Am I addicted? Yeah, in the same sense I’m addicted to eating or breathing or sex. I could stop doing it, but the benefits of doing it far outweigh the downsides. I haven’t seen a downside.”

Talking up the supposed benefits is actually what led to his founding Alp. In an appearance on This Past Weekend with Theo Von in late 2023, the conversation naturally turned to Carlson’s favorite subject. “The truth is, Zyn is a powerful work enhancer,” he told Von before adding, coyly, “and a male enhancer, if you know what I mean.” It was, Carlson would later explain to Politico, a “boner joke,” but watching the clip, and listening to a separate, unrelated podcast with Lex Fridman in which Fridman tells his guest Andrew Huberman, PhD, that Carlson once told him that nicotine “literally…makes my dick super hard,” it’s clear that Carlson isn’t really joking.

To which I say: fine, God bless, whatever works for li’l Tucker, but companies need to be extremely vigilant about someone making public claims about their products, whether that person is a paid spokesperson or not. At the time, Zyn’s application to market its product in the U.S. was still under FDA review, and while the company may not have minded Carlson talking up God massages with the Nelk Boys, the boner thing on Theo Von was a bridge too far. There’s just so much money at stake, and so much money is made by poisoning people and destroying their lives. He’s in a very tough spot. He’s in charge of the largest federal agency, but he’s not in charge, ultimately, of the executive branch.” He declines to speak about Kennedy’s actions around vaccine safety, but he does have something to say about Kennedy’s boss: “I will say that Donald Trump taking the COVID booster, I didn’t see that coming. And I just want to be very clear that I’m disgusted by that.”

As to whether he’s spoken to any friends in the administration—Kennedy or Vice President JD Vance (a friend who also employs Tucker’s son, Buckley)—to expedite Alp’s application with the FDA, he says no. “We shouldn’t need FDA approval. I just want to be clear. I’m not grateful. What people who live mentally in the old America don’t understand is that no one cares about the law anymore. Every convenience store in America is packed with illegal products, and no one’s doing anything about it. It’s filled with Kratom and illegal Chinese vapes. Your average Nilla wafer is, like, 10 times more dangerous than your average nicotine pouch. The whole thing is absurd, and I’m sick of playing along.”

It’s commentary straight out of the Make America Healthy Again playbook, and Tucker’s embrace of nicotine fits neatly into the larger pattern among MAHA types to hate on the FDA, question medical consensus, and recast problematic faves (psychedelics, raw milk, tanning) in a health halo. “With the MAHA thing, there’s of course a lot of bullshit that attaches to any fad, and there are scammers and quack products. But at its core, the idea is: Let’s not tamper with nature too much, and that’s been a bedrock belief of mine since childhood. I don’t take vitamins, and I’ve never supplemented.” (Also, for anyone who watched his 2022 documentary, The End of Men, and remembers its famous exhortation to expose your testicles to red-light therapy in order to increase testosterone production, Carlson can confirm that he has never tanned his balls. “Even the idea of a red light freaks me out. What is that?”)

You may recall that we had originally set out for his hunting and fishing camp, and we spent an hour walking part of the 100-plus acres of land of which Carlson is undisputed lord and master. He shows off the new gas lighting that he just had installed in the main cabin; he fires some of the rifles that make up his small but fearsome battery; he walks me along the banks of a stream where he and his family fly-fish for hours on end. There’s a pristine outhouse and an expansive skeet-shooting range and sweet grave markings for his late, beloved dogs. He seems genuinely at peace—happy, even—as he describes coming here with his wife and kids and extended family. He seems at home. What more could a guy want?

I asked that question to a person who knows more about Tucker Carlson’s career than just about anyone. Jason Zengerle spent four years reporting his new biography of Carlson, Hated by All the Right People, and spoke to dozens of people who’ve witnessed Carlson’s extraordinary journey from CNN, MSNBC, and Fox to the center of the MAGA, MAHA, and manosphere Venn diagram. “I don’t think just being a podcaster is on his agenda,” Zengerle says. “I think being a nicotine pouch mogul is nice, and it’s a good way to stick it to the woke people at Philip Morris, but I think he has greater ambitions.” In 2024, the press had some fun throwing around Carlson’s name as a possible vice presidential candidate for President Trump, and while Carlson has denied having any such aspirations, Zengerle doesn’t buy it. “I think if he plays it correctly, he’s someone who could plausibly be president. He can say all he wants that he doesn’t want to do it. But what else is left for him at this point?”

A reporter attending a Tucker Carlson Live Tour show once described seeing NO ONE CAN CANCEL TUCKER T-shirts in the crowd, and sometimes it seems as if Carlson is actively testing the claim. (Most recently, he hosted far-right flamethrower Nick Fuentes on his podcast and listened passively as his guest slammed “organized Jewry.” He also just told me that female combat Marines are a joke, and we’ll see how that goes over.) If there is something that would turn his legions of listeners and viewers—his followers, really—against him, he hasn’t said it or done it. Not yet, anyway.

Carlson and Patel are bullish on Alp’s growth this year. Patel told me that the brand has a goal of selling 100+ million units by the end of 2026. And after that, who knows? Nicotine is addictive, and so is attention. So are influence and power, and what used to be enough is slowly, then suddenly, no longer enough. And as Carlson knows better than anyone, a person who’s addicted to something will always, always want more.
