What Is the FeNO Test?

FeNO stands for Fractional Exhaled Nitric Oxide. It's a non-invasive, quick way to gauge airway inflammation, especially when eosinophils (a type of white blood cell) drive that inflammation. Key points:

Nitric oxide in the lungs rises when airway cells are irritated or inflamed.

In asthma, this inflammation is often due to allergic triggers or overactive immune responses.

The FeNO test gives doctors a numerical value (usually in parts per billion, ppb) to help understand that inflammation level.

By knowing your FeNO level, your care team gains insight into how active your airway inflammation is—and whether inhaled steroids or other treatments are needed or working well.

How the FeNO Test Is Done

The test itself is fast and painless. Standards from the American Thoracic Society (ATS) and European Respiratory Society (ERS) guide the process, so results are reliable across clinics.

You'll be seated calmly, without talking or eating for a few minutes beforehand.

A soft clip or nose clip may be used to ensure all air comes from your mouth.

You take a deep breath in, then exhale slowly and steadily into the handheld device for about 10 seconds.

The device analyzes your breath and displays a ppb reading within a minute or two.

No blood draws, needles, or radiation—just you breathing normally under guidance.

Why a High FeNO Matters

A high FeNO reading helps your healthcare team in several ways:

Confirms airway inflammation: Supports a diagnosis of asthma when symptoms and spirometry are borderline.

Predicts steroid response: High FeNO often indicates you'll respond well to inhaled corticosteroids, which reduce inflammation.

Monitors treatment: Falling FeNO values over weeks to months show that anti-inflammatory therapy is working.

Guides therapy changes: Persistent high FeNO despite treatment may prompt dosage increases or add-on therapies.

By tailoring treatment to your FeNO levels, doctors aim to control symptoms, reduce flare-ups and keep lung function stable.

Other Causes of Elevated FeNO

While asthma is the most common cause of a high feNo test for asthma meaning, several other factors can elevate nitric oxide levels:

Allergic rhinitis (hay fever) and sinus inflammation

Eosinophilic bronchitis (a non-asthma inflammation)

Atopic dermatitis or other allergic skin conditions

Recent allergen exposure (pollen season, pet dander)

Certain foods rich in nitrates (beetroot, leafy greens—though usually a minor effect)

Always discuss your full medical history and environmental exposures with your doctor to interpret FeNO accurately.

Limitations and Things to Watch For

No test is perfect. Before drawing firm conclusions from your FeNO result, consider:

Smoking: Recent smoking can lower FeNO readings, potentially masking inflammation.

Infections: Viral or bacterial respiratory infections can change NO production.

Technique: Improper breathing technique or not following test instructions can skew results.

Medications: Recent use of oral steroids or nitrate-containing drugs may affect readings.

If you have any doubts about your result, ask your healthcare team for retesting or alternative assessments, like sputum eosinophil counts or bronchial challenge tests.

Clinical Uses of the FeNO Test

According to clinical guidelines, FeNO testing is most useful in:

Diagnosing asthma when symptoms (wheezing, shortness of breath, chest tightness) and spirometry don't give a clear answer.

Assessing eosinophilic airway inflammation to choose or adjust anti-inflammatory treatments.

Monitoring adherence and effectiveness of inhaled corticosteroids over time.

Reducing exacerbations by stepping up preventive therapy before a flare-up becomes severe.

Your doctor will integrate FeNO results with your goals—such as minimizing daily symptoms, avoiding hospital visits, and maintaining normal activity levels.

Next Steps: When to Seek Help

A feNo test for asthma meaning can guide your care, but it doesn't replace medical attention when you need it. Contact your healthcare provider or go to an emergency department if you experience:

Severe shortness of breath at rest

Inability to speak more than a few words without pausing

Bluish lips or fingernails (signs of low oxygen)

Rapid worsening of symptoms despite increased inhaler use

Summary

A feNo test for asthma meaning lies in its ability to measure airway inflammation non-invasively by detecting nitric oxide in your breath. High FeNO levels often point to eosinophilic inflammation—common in asthma—and predict a good response to inhaled steroids. While convenient, the test has limitations and must be interpreted alongside your symptoms, lung function tests, and medical history.

Always:

Discuss any result that concerns you with your healthcare provider

Follow your prescribed asthma action plan

Seek immediate care for life-threatening or rapidly worsening breathing problems

Your doctor is the best source of advice tailored to your situation. Use FeNO as one tool among many to keep your asthma under control and breathe easier.
