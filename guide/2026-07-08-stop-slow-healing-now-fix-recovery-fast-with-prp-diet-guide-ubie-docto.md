What Is PRP and How It Helps

Platelet-Rich Plasma (PRP) concentrates your own platelets and growth factors into a small volume of plasma. When injected or applied topically:

Stimulate new blood vessel formation (angiogenesis)

Enhance collagen synthesis

Recruit stem cells to the injury site

PRP is autologous, minimizing allergy or rejection risk.

Research shows PRP accelerates healing in soft tissue injuries, surgical wounds, and diabetic ulcers.

PRP Treatment Protocol

Preparation

Small blood draw (30–60 mL).

Centrifugation separates PRP from red cells and plasma.

Application

Topical: PRP gel applied directly to a cleaned wound bed.

Injection: Guided by ultrasound for tendons, ligaments, or deep ulcers.

Frequency

Typically 1–3 sessions, spaced 2–4 weeks apart, depending on wound severity.

Safety and Side Effects

Mild discomfort at injection site.

Low risk of infection, as PRP uses your own blood.

Combining PRP and Diet for Best Results

Integrating PRP therapy with targeted nutrition maximizes healing:

Synergy: Growth factors from PRP need protein and micronutrients as building blocks.

Reduced inflammation: A diet rich in omega-3s complements PRP's regenerative signals.

Faster tissue repair: Adequate vitamins and minerals ensure collagen laid down by PRP is strong and organized.

Most clinics recommend starting dietary changes at least 2–4 weeks before PRP and maintaining them throughout the healing process.
