# Mold Toxins Panel (Mycotoxins): Check Exposure

Test details

About the mold toxins test

Mycotoxins are chemical byproducts, including aflatoxins, trichothecenes, ochratoxins, and other toxic compounds such as gliotoxin and zearalenone, produced by certain mold species and excreted in urine after exposure. This test measures their presence directly, which makes it different from a mold allergy test: it assesses toxin load, not your immune system's response. The analysis uses LC-MS/MS (liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry), a technique that detects these compounds at parts-per-trillion sensitivity, significantly more precise than ELISA-based antibody tests. Samples are processed by Mosaic Diagnostics, a CLIA- and CAP-accredited laboratory. From a single first-morning urine sample, you learn which specific mycotoxins are present and at what levels.

Where mold toxin exposure comes from

Mold toxin exposure does not require a visible mold problem or a flooded home. It generally falls into two categories. Environmental exposure happens through water-damaged buildings, damp basements, bathrooms, and HVAC systems, where molds like Stachybotrys ("black mold"), Aspergillus, and Chaetomium can grow. Dietary exposure is just as common and far less obvious: ochratoxin A is frequently found in coffee, dried fruit, grapes, and grains, while aflatoxins turn up in peanuts, corn, and tree nuts. Because many of these foods are everyday staples, exposure can occur gradually and without any obvious source. That's why this panel covers both environmental and food-associated toxins rather than focusing on water damage alone.

This is not a mold allergy test

These two tests measure entirely different things. An IgE mold allergy test measures your immune system's antibody response to mold proteins: it detects sensitivity, not toxin exposure. A mycotoxin test measures the chemical toxins mold produces, directly in your urine, regardless of whether your immune system reacts to them. That distinction matters: someone can have a negative allergy test and still carry a meaningful mycotoxin burden, and the reverse is also true. The two are complementary rather than redundant. If you've already had a mold allergy test, this panel still answers a different question: what toxins are actually present.

Frequently asked questions

What is the difference between a mold allergy test and a mycotoxin test?

They measure entirely different things. A mold allergy test (IgE) measures your immune system's sensitivity to mold proteins, while a mycotoxin test measures the actual chemical toxins mold produces in your urine, regardless of immune response. You can have high toxin exposure with a negative allergy test, which is why one does not replace the other.

Does a positive result mean I have mold in my home?

Not necessarily. Mycotoxins can enter the body through food (coffee, nuts, dried grains, and dried fruit) as well as through water-damaged environments. A positive result indicates that exposure occurred; pinpointing the source requires additional investigation.

Do I need to fast before collecting my urine sample?

Fasting for 12 hours before collection can improve detection accuracy and is recommended per the collection guidelines. First-morning urine is preferred, so collect your first void after waking, following the instructions included in your kit. Connect with your care team if you have questions before your collection day.

How is the creatinine correction applied, and why does it matter?

Urine concentration varies with how hydrated you are, which can make raw toxin readings hard to compare. Creatinine, a waste product excreted at a relatively constant rate, is used as a reference point to normalize results for dilution. This makes your mycotoxin readings more accurate and easier to compare across collections.

How often should I retest?

Retesting frequency depends on your exposure situation and the reason you tested in the first place. Many providers suggest retesting 3-6 months after addressing a known exposure source to assess whether toxin levels have changed. Your care team can help you determine an appropriate timeline.

What does 'LC-MS/MS' mean, and why does it matter?

LC-MS/MS stands for liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry. It is a laboratory technique that identifies and quantifies specific molecules at very low concentrations: in this case, parts per trillion. It is significantly more sensitive and specific than ELISA-based antibody tests, which reduces the risk of false negatives.

Biomarkers tested

Aflatoxin M1

Aflatoxin M1 is formed in the body after ingesting aflatoxin B1, a mycotoxin produced by Aspergillus species that commonly contaminate corn, peanuts, grains, and spices.

Method: LC-MS/MS (Liquid Chromatography/Tandem Mass Spectrometry) measuring free (unconjugated) mycotoxins in urine (CLIA 17D0919496); not cleared or approved by the FDA. Results in ng/g creatinine. Not a stand-alone diagnosis; should be interpreted in clinical context.

Chaetoglobosin A

Chaetoglobosin A is a mold toxin produced by Chaetomium globosum, commonly found on damp drywall, wallpaper, plywood, and ceiling tiles in water-damaged buildings.

Citrinin (Dihydrocitrinone)

Dihydrocitrinone is the primary urinary metabolite of the mycotoxin citrinin, produced by Penicillium and Aspergillus molds.

Enniatin B

Enniatin B is a fat-soluble fungal toxin produced by Fusarium species that colonize moist cereals and plant foods including wheat, oats, maize, apples, coffee, and cocoa.

Gliotoxin

Gliotoxin is a highly bioactive mycotoxin produced primarily by Aspergillus fumigatus, commonly found in water-damaged buildings behind wallpaper, beneath flooring, and inside HVAC systems.

Mycophenolic Acid

Mycophenolic acid is an immunosuppressive fungal metabolite produced by Penicillium species found in water-damaged buildings and certain refrigerated or poorly stored foods.
