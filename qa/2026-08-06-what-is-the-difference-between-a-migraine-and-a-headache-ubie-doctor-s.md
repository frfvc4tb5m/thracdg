Headache vs. Migraine: Key Differences

A typical headache causes dull, pressing pain—often on both sides of the head—with little to no nausea or sensitivity to light and sound. A migraine, by contrast, is a neurological condition marked by moderate-to-severe throbbing pain (usually one-sided) lasting 4–72 hours, frequently accompanied by nausea, light and sound sensitivity, and sometimes visual aura.

Why the distinction matters:

Tension headaches typically respond to OTC pain relievers, hydration, and rest.

Migraines often require prescription medications, preventive therapy, and trigger management.

Red flags (sudden "thunderclap" pain, fever, vision loss, weakness, or confusion) require immediate medical attention.

Because symptoms can overlap and treatment paths differ significantly, identifying what you're experiencing is the critical first step. Understanding the Difference Between a Headache and a Migraine

Headache and migraine are terms people often use interchangeably, but they describe different experiences. Both involve head pain, yet their causes, symptoms, and treatments can vary widely. Knowing these differences can help you manage your discomfort, choose the right treatment, and recognize when to seek medical attention.

What Is a Headache?

A headache is any pain or discomfort in the head or upper neck area. It's one of the most common health complaints:

Types of Headaches

Tension-type headache: Dull, aching pain on both sides; feels like a tight band around the head.

Cluster headache: Severe, burning pain around one eye, often with tearing and nasal congestion.

What Is a Migraine?

A migraine is a specific type of primary headache disorder. It's more than just a bad headache; it's a neurological condition that can impact daily life:

Key Features

Moderate to severe throbbing or pulsating pain, often on one side

Duration: 4 to 72 hours if untreated

Can occur several times a month or less frequently

Associated Symptoms

Nausea and/or vomiting

Sensitivity to light (photophobia) and sound (phonophobia)

Sometimes sensitivity to smells or touch

Visual disturbances (aura) in about 25% of people with migraine

Migraine With Aura vs. Migraine Without Aura

With Aura: Transient neurological symptoms (flashes of light, blind spots, tingling).

Without Aura: No sensory warning signs; headache begins without prelude.

Triggers & Risk Factors

Hormonal changes (menstruation, menopause)

Stress or sudden stress relief

Sleep irregularities

Certain foods (aged cheese, processed meats) and drinks (wine, caffeine)

Environmental factors (bright lights, strong smells)

Family history (genetic predisposition)

Why the Distinction Matters

Treatment Choices

Mild tension-type headaches often respond to over-the-counter (OTC) pain relievers, stress management, and posture correction.

Migraines may require prescription medications (triptans, CGRP inhibitors) and preventative strategies (beta-blockers, anticonvulsants).

Preventive Strategies

Headache prevention focuses on lifestyle changes (hydration, ergonomic work setup).

Migraine prevention may involve identifying triggers, maintaining a headache diary, and daily preventative medications.

Quality of Life

Untreated migraines can interfere with work, school, and social life.

Recognizing migraine early can lead to faster, more targeted relief and fewer lost days.

Any headache that awakens you from sleep

If you experience any of these, call emergency services or go to the nearest emergency department.
