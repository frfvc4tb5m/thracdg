What causes restless legs syndrome

The dopamine connection

RLS is fundamentally a neurological condition. Your brain's dopamine system, the same network involved in movement, motivation, and reward, plays a central role. Dopamine levels naturally fluctuate throughout the day, dropping in the evening. For people with RLS, this decline triggers misfiring signals that create the urge to move.

This explains why restless legs almost always worsen at night. It's not psychological. It's a measurable shift in neurotransmitter activity that your nervous system can't properly regulate.

Primary versus secondary RLS

Primary RLS runs in families. Research suggests a genetic component, with specific gene variants affecting dopamine and iron metabolism in the brain. If a parent had restless legs, your risk increases substantially. Genetic factors influence many sleep disorders.

Secondary RLS has an identifiable trigger: iron deficiency, pregnancy, kidney disease, peripheral neuropathy, or certain medications. Antidepressants (especially SSRIs), antihistamines, and anti-nausea drugs can all provoke or worsen symptoms. Identifying the trigger often leads to effective treatment.

The iron and ferritin connection

Why ferritin matters more than you think

Iron is essential for dopamine production in your brain. Your body stores iron as ferritin, and even when your hemoglobin looks normal on a standard blood test, your ferritin can be low enough to trigger RLS. A study in Sleep Medicine Reviews found that people with RLS frequently have ferritin levels below 50 ng/mL, even though labs typically flag values only below 12 to 15.

This is one of the most common reasons restless legs go untreated for years. Your doctor sees "normal" iron levels and moves on. But your brain's iron stores tell a different story.

How to sleep with restless legs tonight

Immediate relief techniques

When restless legs strike at bedtime, try these evidence-based approaches. A warm bath or shower 60 to 90 minutes before bed raises your core temperature, and the subsequent cooling signals your body to prepare for sleep. The warmth also relaxes muscles and can temporarily quiet RLS sensations.

Compression socks or pneumatic compression devices apply steady pressure that calms the nervous system's urge-to-move signals. A study in Sleep Medicine found pneumatic compression reduced RLS severity by nearly 60% compared to placebo.

Distraction and counter-stimulation

Mental distraction works surprisingly well. Engaging your brain with a moderately challenging task (a crossword, counting backward by threes) can interrupt the sensory loop that makes you focus on leg discomfort. This isn't ignoring the problem. It's redirecting the neural circuits involved.

Counter-stimulation is another option. Rubbing your legs with a textured roller, applying menthol cream, or alternating warm and cool compresses gives your sensory nerves a competing signal. Many people find that managing nerve-related discomfort at bedtime involves similar strategies.

Movement and stretching strategies

Evening stretching routine

Gentle stretching before bed is one of the most consistently helpful strategies for how to sleep with restless legs. Focus on your calves, hamstrings, and hip flexors. Hold each stretch for 30 to 60 seconds, breathing slowly. Avoid vigorous exercise within two hours of bedtime, which can actually intensify symptoms.

Try these stretches nightly:

Standing calf stretch against a wall (30 seconds each side)

Seated hamstring stretch with a towel looped around your foot

Supine figure-four stretch for hip and glute release

Gentle quad stretch lying on your side

Daytime activity matters

Regular moderate exercise during the day reduces RLS severity at night. A study in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine found that consistent aerobic exercise (30 minutes, three times a week) significantly improved RLS symptoms over eight weeks. Walking, swimming, and cycling are particularly effective.

The key word is moderate. Intense evening workouts can backfire, increasing leg restlessness. Excessive movement during sleep often improves when daytime activity becomes more consistent.

Dietary changes that calm restless legs

Nutrients that support dopamine and nerve function

Beyond iron, several nutrients play supporting roles in RLS management. Magnesium helps regulate nerve and muscle function. Low magnesium is common and can contribute to muscle cramping and restlessness. Magnesium supplementation before bed may help some people with RLS, though evidence is limited to smaller studies.

Folate and vitamin B12 support healthy nerve function and methylation pathways. Deficiency in either can worsen neurological symptoms. Vitamin D deficiency has also been associated with more severe RLS in observational studies.

What to avoid in the evening

Caffeine is a significant RLS trigger for many people. It blocks adenosine receptors and can interfere with dopamine regulation. Cut off caffeine by early afternoon, or eliminate it entirely if your symptoms are severe.

Alcohol may seem relaxing, but it fragments sleep and worsens RLS. It disrupts the dopamine system and causes rebound neural excitability as it metabolizes. Refined sugar in the evening can also contribute to blood sugar fluctuations that some RLS sufferers find triggering.

Sleep environment adjustments

Temperature and bedding

Cool legs tend to be calmer legs. Keep your bedroom temperature between 65 and 68 degrees Fahrenheit. Some people find that weighted blankets provide the steady pressure that quiets restless sensations, similar to how compression works.

Breathable, loose-fitting sleepwear prevents overheating. If your legs run hot at night, try sleeping with them outside the covers or using moisture-wicking sheets. The relationship between body temperature and sleep is especially relevant for RLS sufferers.

Consistent sleep timing

A regular sleep schedule helps stabilize your circadian rhythm, which directly influences dopamine cycling. Going to bed and waking up at the same time every day (yes, weekends too) keeps your neurotransmitter patterns more predictable. Sleep deprivation itself worsens RLS, creating a vicious cycle that consistent timing helps break.

When to see a doctor about RLS

Signs it's time for professional help

If restless legs disrupt your sleep more than three nights a week, affect your daytime functioning, or don't respond to lifestyle changes within a few weeks, it's time to see a clinician. Severe RLS is a recognized medical condition with effective prescription treatments.

Also see a doctor if your symptoms started after beginning a new medication, if you have heart palpitations or other cardiovascular symptoms, or if you notice involuntary leg jerking during sleep (periodic limb movement disorder, which often accompanies RLS).

Treatment options your doctor may offer

For iron deficiency-related RLS, your doctor may recommend high-dose oral iron or IV iron infusions when oral supplementation isn't raising ferritin fast enough. For dopamine-related RLS, medications like gabapentin enacarbil or low-dose dopamine agonists can reduce symptoms significantly.

Alpha-2-delta ligands (gabapentin, pregabalin) are now recommended as first-line treatments over dopamine agonists for many patients, as they carry less risk of a phenomenon called augmentation, where dopamine-based medications eventually make RLS worse. Your treatment plan should be individualized based on your lab values, symptom severity, and medical history.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main cause of restless legs syndrome?

The primary cause involves dysfunction in the brain's dopamine system, often linked to low iron stores. Dopamine helps regulate movement signals, and when brain iron is insufficient for proper dopamine production, the misfiring creates the urge-to-move sensations characteristic of RLS. Genetics, pregnancy, kidney disease, and certain medications can also trigger or worsen symptoms.

How to sleep with restless legs during pregnancy?

RLS affects up to 25% of pregnant women, often due to dropping iron and folate levels. Talk to your OB about checking ferritin levels. Gentle evening stretches, warm baths, and leg massages are safe during pregnancy. Avoid antihistamine-based sleep aids, which can worsen symptoms. Most pregnancy-related RLS resolves within a few weeks after delivery.

Does magnesium help with restless legs?

Magnesium may help some people with RLS, particularly those with low magnesium levels. It supports nerve and muscle relaxation. Magnesium glycinate or citrate taken before bed are commonly recommended forms. However, evidence specifically for RLS is limited to small studies. It's most effective as part of a broader approach that includes iron optimization and lifestyle changes.

Can restless legs syndrome be cured?

Secondary RLS caused by iron deficiency, medication side effects, or pregnancy can often be fully resolved by treating the underlying cause. Primary (genetic) RLS is typically managed rather than cured, but effective treatments exist. Many people achieve significant symptom reduction through iron supplementation, lifestyle modifications, and when needed, prescription medications.

What makes restless legs worse at night?

Dopamine levels naturally decline in the evening as part of your circadian rhythm. For people with RLS, this drop unmasks the sensory and motor symptoms that dopamine normally suppresses. Inactivity and lying down also contribute, as reduced sensory input allows your brain to focus on the out of range signals coming from your legs.

Should I take iron supplements for restless legs?

Only if your ferritin level is below 75 ng/mL. Taking iron without testing can lead to iron overload, which carries its own health risks. Get a ferritin test first. If supplementation is warranted, take iron with vitamin C on an empty stomach for better absorption. Your doctor may recommend IV iron if oral supplements don't raise your levels adequately.

Does exercise help or hurt restless legs?

Moderate daytime exercise consistently helps reduce RLS severity. Walking, swimming, and cycling for 30 minutes three to five times a week show the most benefit. However, intense exercise close to bedtime can worsen symptoms. Aim to finish vigorous activity at least two to three hours before sleep, and use gentle stretching as part of your bedtime routine.

Can certain medications cause restless legs?

Yes. SSRIs and SNRIs (antidepressants), diphenhydramine and other antihistamines, anti-nausea medications like metoclopramide, and some antipsychotics can trigger or worsen RLS. If your symptoms started or worsened after beginning a new medication, talk to your prescriber about alternatives. Never stop a medication without medical guidance.
